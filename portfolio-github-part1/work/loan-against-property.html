Product design · Lending FinBox for Muthoot Fincorp

One property loan, eleven stages, one shared workspace

A Loan Against Property passes through sales, credit, legal, technical and operations teams before any money moves. At FinBox, I designed the internal lending platform and the Relationship Officer’s field app for Muthoot Fincorp. Now all of those teams work from the same case, instead of from their own spreadsheets, calls and inboxes.

The problem

You can’t decide a property loan from data alone

A digital personal loan can often be decided in minutes from identity, credit and bank data. A property loan also needs answers no API holds. Who really owns this property? Is the title clean? Is it already mortgaged? What is it worth?

Those answers come from people and outside agencies, and they take days. One case can carry co-applicants, guarantors and several owners, and the document list grows with each person, each income type and each property.

A better form wouldn’t fix that. The real problem was coordination: work got lost between teams, and nobody could say who was holding a case.

So the goal became: make a long, human process visible, so everyone knows what they own and what happens next.

The approach

Five layers on one case record

Two rules shaped the structure: customers shouldn’t do heavy work before they know they can proceed, and the lender can’t make a real offer until verification is done. Together they split the journey into five layers, each designed for the people who work in it: Qualify, Apply, Verify, Offer and Disburse.

Underneath sits one case record and a shared eleven-stage model. Credit approval is only the sixth stage, so sanction was never the finish line. There’s as much work after it as before it.

01

Qualify: quick data entry in one visit

The RO and the customer, at the customer’s door, on the RO’s phone. Quick data entry asks only what decides the next step: mobile number, loan product, source, PAN, a few basic details, and the property’s size and type.

Design call: consent stays with the customer. The RO runs the app, but the customer never fills in a form. They confirm their number with a code, approve the Aadhaar check through DigiLocker and give bureau consent from links on their own phone, so their data and their consent stay theirs.

Design call: no loan amount yet. The property hasn’t been assessed, and an early number would set an expectation the valuation could break. Once consent is in, the RO sends a payment link and the customer pays the login fee (₹1,180 in the sample). The case moves to detailed data entry.

Failure states got the same care as the happy path. A flagged existing customer sees a plain “can’t go ahead right now” that says it isn’t a loan decision and who can help. Aadhaar checks can be retried, and after five failures the RO scans the card instead, masked automatically. Every code and link shows a countdown, so nobody is left guessing.

02

Apply: one checklist per case

The RO, in the field. A checklist, not a long form: one Complete Checklist screen per case, with a card for every applicant and co-applicant, each property, the liability form, a selfie with the customer and two references.

The form adapts to the case. Salaried applicants search for their company or add it. Self-employed applicants fetch business details from GST, or from Udyam without GST. Marital status and single or joint ownership change what’s asked next. Co-applicants and guarantors are added separately, because their liability is different.

Returned work stays visible. Queries and send-backs from credit get their own tabs, so they don’t get buried under new leads.

Evidence is captured where it exists. Property details take five short steps, and title documents are checked on upload. Photos and the selfie carry location and time. The liability form starts from the bureau report, so the customer only confirms what’s there.

03

Verify: a case page built for reviewing evidence

Credit Officers, BPO reviewers and agencies work here. A Credit Officer’s work is closer to an investigation than a form, so the page is built for that. The applicant stays in view: a fixed left rail holds status, loan ID and branch, and tabs hold underwriting, co-applicants, KYC, documents, verification and review.

Each check is a tracked task, showing who it applies to, its status and when it was resolved. Design call: every person is a separate risk. Personal discussion and tele verification repeat for the applicant, co-applicant and guarantor, not once per case.

Exceptions stay on the record. Queries go back to the RO. Deviations and sanction conditions sit under Manual Credit Review.

  1. 1Your work stays apart from other people’s. My Verifications is separate from Other Verifications, which holds RCU, legal and technical reports owned by others.
  2. 2New Verification lets an officer keep a check or assign it. BPO reviewers answer structured questionnaires in their own queue.
  3. 3Underwriting in one tab: credit policy, the bureau report, bank statements and GST or Udyam data, with a marker showing whether each statement came through Bank Connect or offline.
04

Offer: one current offer, with a record of how it changed

The offer arrives only after verification and credit review, in the customer’s app, with the sanctioned amount, rate, tenure, EMI and charges.

Design call: negotiation is a request, not a promise. When a customer pushes back, the request moves from sales to credit, which checks it against policy. Each request has an owner and a status, so sales can’t promise what credit hasn’t approved.

A worked example. Offered ₹20,00,000, the customer asks for ₹25,00,000. The Branch Business Manager raises the request, it goes to the Branch Credit Manager (BCM) with a remark and a linked document, and credit approves ₹22,00,000 at 18% for 120 months, an EMI of ₹39,641. The customer gets more than the first offer but less than the ask, which is why a request can never be treated as a promise.

05

Disburse: releasing money in one payment or several

Credit and operations release a sanctioned loan in one payment or in tranches. The Disbursal tab shows the sanction amount, loan-to-value ratio and charges, then tracks each payment through to operations.

A guided three-step release: amount and date, then deductions, then payment mode. Every deduction is listed, so the net amount is clear before anything is sent.

Each step has its own status. Penny drop (a ₹1 check that the bank account is real), eSign and eNACH (the auto-debit mandate for EMIs) are tracked separately, and a stalled step can be resumed or updated by hand.

The key design decision

Organize daily work around the next task, not the application

First direction: an application list filtered by stage. It mirrored the system, so it felt natural. Putting it in front of officers showed the flaw: it told them what was in the system, but not what to do next. With eleven stages and several open tasks on each case, they would have had to keep their own to-do list in their heads.

Final direction: I moved the daily view from applications to tasks. My Queue gives each person their own list, grouped by task type, with enough context to act without hunting through dashboards.

  1. 1My Cases holds assigned work, Pool holds work that still needs an owner, and All Cases gives the wider view.
  2. 2Counts on each task type show where work is piling up.
  3. 3A case appears in your queue when it’s your turn, with the task you need to finish.
Across the hierarchy

The same list, at every level

Business and credit run as parallel ladders, from front line to national level. Each level needs a wider view, but none needs a separate product.

So the list stays the same and only its scope changes. Branch-level roles, such as Credit Officers and Branch Credit Managers, have no branch filter. Cluster, state and zonal managers look across many branches, so they get one. National credit adds product, date and past-application filters.

Outcome and what’s next

A shared structure for a manual process

The platform is live. Every team, from the RO at the customer’s door to national credit, now works from one case, one stage model and a queue of what’s theirs to do next.

If I kept going, I’d make time visible in every queue, so delays show up before cases are late; show why a case is blocked, not just that it is; and give the low-valuation path the same attention as the happy path.

Reflection · What I learned
  • In an operations product, you’re designing the handoffs. The screens matter less than what happens between them.
  • The most useful question about a workflow is “who is waiting on whom?” It surfaced more problems than any user flow did.
  • Compliance rules are design material. RBI’s Key Facts Statement, for example, decides what a borrower must see before signing.
  • Test the obvious structure early. My first instinct, a list that mirrored the system, mirrored the database, not the job. Officers showed me that faster than any review would have.
