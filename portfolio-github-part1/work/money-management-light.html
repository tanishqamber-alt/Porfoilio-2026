Money Management in Kinnect

Turning scattered financial records into one connected view of a family’s wealth.

RoleProduct Designer
ProductKinnect
UsersFamily office professionals
ScopeFour connected financial pillars: net worth, income and budget, cash flow, ownership
ProblemA family’s wealth is spread across members, accounts, trusts and entities, and they’re linked to each other. No single view showed the whole position.
What I didI mapped how the pieces relate, then designed four pillars that read from the same data, with shared patterns that link every total to its records.

OutcomeOne financial home where professionals can see a family’s whole position, then drop into the detail behind any number without switching tools.
Introduction

A financial home for family offices

Kinnect is an AI-powered platform that family office professionals use to manage the wealth and personal affairs of high-net-worth families.

Money Management is where the financial side comes together: what a family owns and owes, what comes in and goes out, and who holds what. My goal was to make it feel like one connected experience, not four separate tools.

The problem

Family wealth doesn’t live in one place

One family can involve several members, dozens of accounts, businesses, trusts, assets and liabilities, each with its own records and ownership structure.

The harder part is how they relate. A transaction sits in an account. The account may belong to an entity. That entity may be held by a trust, with several family members behind it.

Family office professionals need two things at once: the whole picture, meaning where the family stands, how it’s changing and who owns what; and the detail behind it, meaning the records that explain any number, without starting over in another tool.

The design challenge

How might we make complex family wealth easy to understand without hiding its complexity?

Another report wouldn’t answer that. The numbers had to stay connected to the accounts, entities and activity behind them, so any total could be explained in one or two clicks.

My role

What I owned

Information architectureI mapped how members, trusts, entities, accounts and transactions relate, and turned that into the four-pillar structure.
Pillar designI designed the net worth, income and transactions, cash flow and ownership experiences.
Shared patternsI defined what holds the pillars together: linked totals, shared filters and in-context alerts.

What made it hard

The complexity is real.Ownership chains and dozens of accounts can’t be simplified away without losing the truth.
The transaction register has to be trustworthy.Income, budget and spending figures are built from transactions, so if the data there is messy, everything built on it suffers.
Data entry is the bottleneck.Records come from statements and agreements, and typing them in by hand is slow.
The approach

Four pillars, one question each, all on the same records

Before designing screens, I mapped how the pieces of family finance relate. Everything in Money Management falls under four pillars, and each answers a single question.

01

Net Worth: one position, with the story behind it

Net worth is the widest view, where assets and liabilities come together into one consolidated position.

A single total hides a lot, so I placed it beside the views that explain it.

Each answers a different question, so the challenge was keeping them readable side by side without flattening them into one number.

  1. 1Historical values show how wealth has changed.
  2. 2Concentration and liquidity show how it’s held and how quickly it can be used.
  3. 3Ownership distribution and debt exposure show who holds it and what’s owed against it.
02

Income, Budget & Transactions: a register you can trust

Income Trends is where a family’s spending story starts: what came in, what went out, and which categories moved against a normal month.

  1. 1Gaps are counted, not hidden. Uncategorized transactions sit next to the total, so they show up before they skew a report.
  2. 2Last month against the 6-month average shows which categories moved, and by how much.
  3. 3A 6-month trend per category separates a one-off spike from a real shift.

The register behind every number

Transactions brings every connected account into one table, with eight filters: vendor, date, account, category, linked template, amount, status and tag.

  1. 1Each row keeps its context: account, category, linked cash flow template, tags and comments.
  2. 2An arrow on each category shows whether money came in or went out, without adding a column.
  3. 3System-created tags for entities and properties let people group spending by what it belongs to.

Rules for repeat work

Transaction rules handle repeat work. Instead of categorizing the same transactions every month, people decide once.

A rule reads like a sentence: if a transaction matches these criteria, apply these changes. Only the parts that are switched on open up, and one checkbox applies the rule to past transactions.

03

Cash Flow: where money is going, and whether there’ll be enough

Net worth tells you where a family stands. Cash flow tells you how money is moving, and whether there will be enough when it’s needed.

It has three views: Projected Cash Flows looks ahead twelve months, Cash Movement shows what has already moved, and Plan vs. Actual compares the two.

  1. 1The four totals read as one sum. Cash balance $1,878,807 + net cash flows $32,502 − capital reserves $101,002 = projected available cash $1,810,307.
  2. 2Each total links to what’s behind it: 15 accounts, 29 cash flow records and 4 reserves.
  3. 3Minimum and maximum alerts flag accounts outside their limits, such as a foundation account $100,000 below its minimum.

Down to the account

Design call: drawers over new pages. When someone needs the activity behind a cash flow, the account register opens in a drawer over Cash Movement, so they don’t lose their place.

  1. 1The period reconciles before the first row: beginning balance, deposits, withdrawals and ending balance.
  2. 2It opens already filtered to the account, with the same filters as Transactions.
  3. 3Deposits are green, so money coming in stands out in a long list.
04

Ownership & Entity: who owns what

Individuals, trusts, businesses and legal entities are all tied to a family’s money, and ownership is what links them to the numbers.

The Ownership Map puts the whole structure in one view. Every person, trust, charity and entity is a card with its net assets, its records and its owners, and the lines between cards carry each owner’s share.

  1. 1Colour says what kind of holder a card is: people, trusts, entities and charities. Each line ends in the owner’s share.
  2. 2Recent changes are flagged on the card, such as the Dynasty Trust’s stake in Morrison Family LP rising to 70% in November 2026.
  3. 3The time scrubber replays the structure through 15 ownership changes since 2017. Past today it shows planned changes as dashed cards.

Money on the same map

Design call: show cash flows on the ownership map, not in another report. A setting draws a year of planned or actual transfers between the cards, so the money and the structure that explains it are read together.

  1. 1The summary splits the year’s money three ways: into the family $792K, out of the family $348K, and between members $886K.
  2. 2Pointing at a card fades everything it isn’t connected to, so one person’s money can be followed: Kyle Morrison, in $269K and out $384K.
  3. 3Plan and actual use the same lines, dotted for planned and solid for actual, and each card shows the gap between them.
AI & automation

Less manual entry, same human control

Document-to-data turns statements and agreements into structured records, with AI suggesting where they belong and people deciding what’s saved.

The next case study, AI & Automation in Kinnect, covers this in depth.

Key decisions

Structure by question, and connect every number

01Structure by question, not by data type.Each pillar answers one question, so people know where to go without learning the data model.
02Every total opens its records.A number you can’t explain is a number you can’t trust. Totals link to what’s behind them, and the activity behind a cash flow opens in an account register.
03One set of filters everywhere.Transactions and every account register share the same eight filters, so people learn them once.
04Show gaps where people already work.Uncategorized counts, Items to Resolve badges on records that need attention, and balance alerts sit on the main screens, not on a separate exceptions page.
How it fits together

One connected view of family wealth

Four views read the same records, in order. Transactions add up to cash flow, cash flow moves net worth, and ownership shows whose net worth it is. AI and automation keep those records filled in.

Outcome and what’s next

One financial home

Money Management is one financial home: any number on any pillar can be traced back to the records behind it.

If I kept going, I’d push Plan vs. Actual further, so variances explain themselves instead of waiting for someone to investigate them.

Reflection

What this project taught me

  • Give complexity structure instead of hiding it. Simplifying family wealth would have made it wrong.
  • Information architecture is the product’s logic. It decides what connects to what, long before any screen exists.
  • A chart earns its place when it answers one clear question. Anything else belongs in a table.
  • The links between workflows need as much design attention as the workflows themselves.
